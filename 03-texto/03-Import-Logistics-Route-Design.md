 Import Logistics Route Design: Frozen Boneless Chicken Breast from Brazil to Apodaca
                                         (PROBOCA, Case 2)
       This document is the third part of our Import Manual for PROBOCA. Part 1 chose Brazil
as the origin for Case 2 (Valdez Gómez et al., 2026a), and the Supplier Selection and Evaluation
Manual ranked seven Brazilian suppliers that own plants authorized by Mexico's food-safety
authority (Servicio Nacional de Sanidad, Inocuidad y Calidad Agroalimentaria [SENASICA],
2026). Here we design one import logistics route for each of those seven suppliers, compare their
cost, lead time, complexity and risk, test two simple financial scenarios, and recommend the
most efficient and sustainable option.
       Unit of analysis. Every route moves the same load, so the strategies can be compared on
equal terms: one 40-ft high-cube reefer container with 26 t net of frozen boneless skinless
chicken breast at −18 °C, from the supplier's plant to PROBOCA's plant in Apodaca, Nuevo
León. At the September 2026 export price of US$2,487 per metric ton (Ministério do
Desenvolvimento, Indústria, Comércio e Serviços [MDIC], 2026), the load is worth US$64,662
FOB. Each supplier ships from the plant named in its request for quotation (RFQ).
       What is fact and what is estimate. Distances, official tariffs and legal rules come from
primary sources and are cited. Ocean freight, Mexican trucking, insurance premiums and some

port charges have no public lane-specific price, so we use clearly labeled estimates (Appendix A)
that will be replaced by real quotes from carriers and forwarders. Because the ocean leg and the

Mexican leg are the same for all seven suppliers, those estimates move every total by the same
amount and do not change the ranking. The ranking depends on what really differs: the distance

from each plant to its port and the number of hand-offs on the way.
       Why timing matters. Brazilian chicken enters Mexico duty-free only for importers in

the basic-basket import registry and only until December 31, 2026, unless the decree is renewed;
otherwise the duty is 75% (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Foreign Agricultural Service [USDA
                                                                                                     3

FAS], 2026). The duty that applies is the one in force on the day the vessel anchors at the
Mexican port (Ley Aduanera, art. 56, 2026). A logistics delay can therefore cost far more in duty

than in freight, and Section 3 measures that effect.
                            1. Logistics Route Strategies by Supplier

       The seven routes share the same structure: a refrigerated truck (or train) to a port in
southern Brazil, a reefer container on a liner service to Altamira, customs and sanitary clearance

at Altamira, and a refrigerated truck to Apodaca. Section 1.1 sets the elements that are common
to all routes; Section 1.2 gives the strategy for each supplier; Section 1.3 estimates the cost of

each one.
1.1 Common Design for All Seven Routes

       Transport mode and service provider. Table 1 shows the mode and type of provider

chosen for each leg. Ocean reefer container is the only realistic mode for a frozen load of 26 t
over about 10,600 km: air freight would cost close to the value of the cargo, and ocean transport

has the lowest emissions per tonne-kilometer of any mode in the chain (Department for Energy
Security and Net Zero [DESNZ], 2025). The ocean service is Maersk's UHA-GULFEX

northbound loop, which calls at Itajaí and Paranaguá and reaches Altamira in 30 days from either
port (Maersk, n.d.). The same vessel calls at Veracruz about eight days earlier, which gives a

contingency port (Section 3).

          Route and customs offices. The customs office of exit is the Brazilian Federal Revenue

customs house at the port of loading: the Alfândega da Receita Federal do Brasil no Porto de

       Packaging, packing and labeling. We propose a three-level design that keeps the cold

chain intact and reduces waste:
           Primary: two 10-kg bags of food-grade, single-material polyethylene per carton,

           which can be recycled; no PVC or mixed laminates.

           Secondary: a wax-free corrugated master carton, 20 kg net, made from recycled or

           certified fiber. Wax-coated cartons resist moisture but cannot be recycled, so we ask
           for a repulpable water-resistant coating.

           Tertiary: 20 heat-treated wood pallets marked under ISPM 15, which Mexico

           requires for wood packaging (Secretaría de Medio Ambiente y Recursos Naturales

           [SEMARNAT], 2017). Heat treatment is preferred to methyl-bromide fumigation,
           which damages the ozone layer. If PROBOCA's dock has push-pull forklifts, slip-

           sheets replace the pallets and remove about 440 kg of wood per container. Loads are

           secured with PET straps and corner boards instead of full stretch wrap.

           Labels on every carton: product name in Spanish and Portuguese, plant name and
           SIF number with the federal inspection stamp, lot, production and expiry dates, net

           weight, storage temperature (−18 °C), country of origin, and a GS1-128 barcode with

           lot, dates and weight for traceability. The label must match the sanitary certificate

           exactly; a mismatch is the most common reason for a hold at the port. Because the
           product is industrial raw material and not a consumer package, the customs broker

           confirms before the first shipment which labeling rules apply.

       Cubic capacity and space utilization. Table 2 shows the load plan. In a frozen-meat

container, weight—not volume—is the limit: 26 t fills 89% of the container's payload but only

76% of its usable volume, and the free space is the air channel the reefer needs to keep the load
at −18 °C. The plan therefore loads to the weight limit, keeps the cargo below the red load line,

and leaves the T-floor and the top clear.

3.1 Freight Increase of 15% to 20%
          A 15% rise adds 9.9% to 10.6% to the total logistics cost, and a 20% rise adds 13.2% to
14.1%. The ranking does not change, and the gap between the best and the worst route widens:
Seara's advantage over C.Vale grows from US$1,651 to US$1,897 (+15%) and US$1,979
(+20%) per container, because the suppliers with longer road legs pay the increase on more

kilometers. Ocean freight explains between half and two-thirds of the increase for every supplier,
so the best protection is a fixed-rate contract with the carrier, not the choice of supplier.

3.2 Transit Delay of One Week
        In normal months a one-week delay is cheap: US$634 per container, about 5% of the
logistics cost, and delivery moves from 39–40 to 46–47 days. The same delay becomes very
expensive if it pushes the vessel's anchoring at Altamira past December 31, 2026 and the duty
exemption is not renewed. Then the 75% duty applies to the customs value of US$69,542, which

adds US$52,157 per container—between 4.2 and 4.9 times the entire logistics cost of the route
(Ley Aduanera, art. 56, 2026). Freight, therefore, is not the variable to watch in the last quarter of
2026: the calendar is.

                                    4. Executive Recommendation
        We recommend that PROBOCA use the Seara (JBS) route as its main logistics
strategy for Brazilian boneless chicken breast, with Aurora Coop as the second source. The
main route is: Seara's Lapa plant (SIF 530) → 151 km by truck to TCP Paranaguá → 40-ft reefer
on the Maersk UHA-GULFEX loop (or the cheaper of two carriers quoted) → Aduana de

Altamira with SENASICA inspection → 492 km by 6-axle refrigerated truck to Apodaca.
        Why this route, in numbers:
            Most efficient. US$10,725 per container, or 41.3 US cents per kg: US$1,114 to
            US$1,651 less than any other supplier, equal to US$43–63 per ton of product.
            Most sustainable. 6.76 t CO₂e per container, 15% to 20% less than the other routes

            by road, and still lower than the three rail options (6.82–7.09 t). The short road leg is
            what makes the difference: it is the only part of the chain that changes between
            suppliers.
            Fastest and simplest. 39 days door to door, the only route that reaches the port the
            same day it is loaded, and the lowest complexity score (2 of 5).

            Most resilient. Seara has authorized plants in five states, so a sanitary closure of
            Paraná does not stop supply. The advantage holds when freight rises 15% or 20%, and
            it widens.
            Consistent with the supplier evaluation. Seara and Aurora were tied at the top of
            the weighted supplier ranking (73.4 and 73.6 of 100); logistics breaks the tie in
            Seara's favor.

        Why Aurora as the second source. Aurora ships from another state (Santa Catarina) and
another port (Itajaí) on the same vessel loop, so it covers the two risks the main route cannot
cover alone: a sanitary closure of Paraná and a closure of Paranaguá. As a guide, we suggest
about two of every three containers from Seara and one from Aurora; PROBOCA decides the
final split with the real quotes.

        Sustainability pilot. Lar Cooperativa by rail from Cascavel reaches 6.82 t CO₂e, almost
as low as Seara, and Lar is a cooperative of more than 15,000 farming families. We propose one
pilot container once the rail rate is quoted, if the rail leg costs no more than the road leg
(US$1,938).

        What would change this recommendation:
         1. A supplier whose FOB quote is lower than Seara's by more than its gap per ton in

                                           Appendix B
                                       Calculation Method
       The model is in the workbook PROBOCA_Case2_Logistics_Cost_Model.xlsx; every
estimate is an input cell, so the totals update when real quotes arrive. The formulas, with Seara as
the worked example (amounts in USD), are:
           Brazilian haulage = (2 × road km × 7.4986 + 662.01) ÷ 4.9698. Seara: (2 × 151 ×
           7.4986 + 662.01) ÷ 4.9698 = 589.

           Insurance = 0.005 × 1.10 × (64,662 + 4,500) = 380.
           Customs value = FOB value + ocean freight + insurance = 69,542. DTA = 0.008 ×
           69,542 = 556.
           Carrying cost = 64,662 × 6.7358% × lead-time days ÷ 360. Seara: 39 days = 472.
           Duty if the vessel anchors after December 31, 2026 without renewal = 0.75 ×
           69,542 = 52,157.
           Emissions = gross cargo of 27.61 t × the sum of (km × factor) for each leg. Seara:
           27.61 × (151 × 0.11417 + 10,627 × 0.01612 + 492 × 0.11417) = 6,757 kg CO₂e.
           Scenarios multiply the three transport legs by 1.15 or 1.20 and recalculate insurance
           and DTA; the one-week delay adds seven days of carrying cost and seven days of
           reefer power at the origin terminal.

       The emission factors are the UK government's averages and do not include the extra
energy a reefer container uses at sea, so absolute emissions are understated. Because the same
factors are applied to all seven routes, the comparison between suppliers remains valid, as
required by ISO 14083 (ISO, 2023).
